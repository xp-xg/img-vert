What is WebP?

WebP is a modern image format developed by Google and released in 2010. It was designed specifically for the web, with one goal in mind: make image files smaller without making them look worse.

The short answer to "what is WebP format?" is this: it's a replacement for JPG, PNG, and GIF that delivers the same visual quality at significantly smaller file sizes. Smaller files mean faster websites, lower bandwidth bills, and better Google PageSpeed scores.

In practical terms, a WebP image is typically:

  • 25–34% smaller than a comparable JPEG
  • 26% smaller than a comparable PNG (lossless mode)
  • Capable of replacing GIFs for simple animations at a fraction of the file size

How WebP compression works

WebP achieves its small file sizes through two compression modes:

Lossy compression (like JPEG, but better)

WebP lossy compression uses a technique called block prediction, borrowed from the VP8 video codec. It analyses groups of pixels and predicts what the neighbouring pixels should look like, then only stores the difference. This is far more efficient than JPEG's DCT (Discrete Cosine Transform) approach, which is why WebP lossy files are significantly smaller at equivalent quality settings.

Lossless compression (like PNG, but smaller)

WebP lossless uses a combination of techniques including spatial prediction, colour transformation, and entropy coding. The result is that a lossless WebP file — one where no pixel data is discarded — is still 26% smaller than an equivalent PNG.

Transparency and animation support

Unlike JPEG, WebP supports alpha channel transparency (like PNG). Unlike PNG, it also supports animation (like GIF). This makes WebP the only modern format that can replace all three legacy formats in most use cases.

Browser support in 2026

One of the most common questions about WebP is whether it works everywhere. The answer in 2026 is a confident yes.

  • Chrome — Full support since 2014
  • Firefox — Full support since 2019
  • Safari — Full support since Safari 14 (2020)
  • Edge — Full support since 2018
  • Opera — Full support since 2013
  • Samsung Internet — Full support since 2016

WebP now has over 96% global browser support. The only remaining exceptions are very old browsers (Internet Explorer 11, which has less than 0.5% market share) and some niche environments. For virtually all practical web publishing purposes, you can use WebP without a fallback.

When to use WebP — and when not to

Use WebP for:

  • All images on websites and web apps
  • Blog post images, product photos, thumbnails
  • Icons and UI graphics (use lossless mode)
  • Improving Google PageSpeed scores
  • Reducing hosting bandwidth costs
  • Social media posts (most platforms now accept WebP)

Stick with JPG or PNG for:

  • Print workflows — most printers and print software expect JPG or TIFF
  • Professional photo editing — RAW, TIFF, or PSD for maximum quality
  • Compatibility with older desktop applications that don't support WebP
  • Email attachments — some email clients don't render WebP inline

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between WebP lossy and WebP lossless?

Lossy WebP discards some pixel data to achieve the smallest possible file size — ideal for photos where minor quality reduction is acceptable. Lossless WebP preserves every pixel exactly but still achieves files 26% smaller than PNG — ideal for logos, screenshots, and graphics where precision matters.

Does WebP work in all browsers?

Yes. As of 2026, WebP is supported by all major browsers including Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge, and Opera, covering over 96% of global users.

Is WebP better than JPG?

For web use, yes. WebP lossy files are 25–34% smaller than equivalent JPEGs at comparable quality. WebP also supports transparency and animation, which JPG does not.

Can I convert WebP back to JPG?

Yes. Use img-vert's free WebP to JPG converter — it works instantly in your browser with no upload required.

What is the difference between JPG and JPEG?

None. JPG and JPEG are the same format. JPEG stands for Joint Photographic Experts Group. The shorter .jpg extension became common on Windows, which originally required 3-letter file extensions.

Is WebP good for SEO?

Yes. Google's PageSpeed Insights specifically recommends serving images in next-gen formats like WebP. Faster page load times correlate with better Google rankings, especially on mobile.
